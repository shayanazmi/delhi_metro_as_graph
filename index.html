Delhi Metro as a map of connections

A graph is just dots joined by lines. Here, every dot is a metro station and every line is a stretch of track. With this one idea, maths can answer real questions: How are stations connected? What is the quickest way? What happens if something breaks?

The skeleton of the network

A spanning tree keeps every station reachable using the fewest links, with no loops.

Try it
  1. Press Build spanning tree.
  2. Faded tracks are the ones that would close a loop.
  3. Press Build with Prim to grow a tree a different way, and compare the totals.
Press the button.
Takeaway. A tree of n stations always has exactly n−1 links. The extra links are the loops, which give the network its backup routes. This is a thought experiment, not a plan to remove real tracks.

What you now know

  • A graph is dots (vertices) joined by lines (edges). The Metro is one.
  • Degree is how many neighbours a dot has.
  • BFS finds the fewest stops; Dijkstra finds the quickest time.

  • Bridges and cut stations show where there is no second route.

Glossary

How this was built

The data is the official Delhi Metro timetable (GTFS format): 262 stops, weekday trips only. Two stations are joined when they appear next to each other in a trip, and each link is weighted by its median scheduled time. Station coordinates are used only to draw the map, never to decide who is a neighbour. One link, Aqua Line Sector 51 to Blue Line Sector 52, was added from public interchange information with an assumed 5-minute walk.

What this model cannot tell you: how crowded a station is, where trains are right now, or how long a change really takes. Adjacency comes from consecutive stops in timetabled trips. The timetable ended on 31 Dec 2025, and one link (Aqua Line Sector 51 to Blue Line Sector 52) comes from public interchange information.

★Behind the project

The case study

The short version of the written report: what we asked, how we built the graph, what we found, and what we cannot claim.

1. The research question

How can graph theory help us understand the Delhi Metro's connectivity, compare routes, identify structurally important stations, and analyse the effect of losing connections?

  1. Which stations are structurally important?
  2. How do algorithms find and compare journeys?
  3. Which connections and stations are critical to connectivity?
  4. How do sorting algorithms compare when ranking stations?
  5. What does the graph reveal, and not reveal, about the real Metro?
2. Data and method

Source: the Delhi Metro timetable in GTFS format. It has 262 stops, 12 routes and 5,438 trips (5,379 on weekdays). Its calendar ends on 31 Dec 2025. There is no transfers file, so we did not invent transfer times.

Rule: two stations are neighbours only if they appear one after the other in a trip. Never because they are close on a map, and never alphabetically.

  1. Keep weekday trips.
  2. Join each pair of consecutive stops with an edge.
  3. Merge duplicates across trips and directions. Weight = median scheduled seconds.
  4. Clean names, keep raw names.
  5. Check: no gaps in stop order, no self-loops, no zero or negative times, every link present in both directions.

One external link: the feed leaves the Aqua Line (21 stations) disconnected. Public sources confirm an interchange between Aqua Sector 51 and Blue Sector 52, so we added one edge with an assumed 5-minute walk. We checked three other nearby pairs and did not add them for lack of evidence.

3. Graph definition and algorithms

Simple, undirected, weighted graph. Vertex = station. Edge = direct link. Weight = median scheduled seconds (300 s for the added walkway).

5. Validation
  • The website's JavaScript gave the same bridge (122), cut-station (117) and component (1) counts as the Python and NetworkX reference.
  • Degrees sum to twice the edges, and selection sort made exactly n(n−1)/2 comparisons.
  • Not done: a line-by-line comparison with DMRC's official map.
6. Limitations
  • The timetable is static, not live train positions, and it has expired.
  • Coordinates draw the map; they never decide adjacency.
  • Degree and betweenness do not prove crowding.
  • No transfer times, so journey minutes are in-vehicle only.
  • A few Rapid Metro segments vary a lot (180 to 720 s).
  • One link and its walking time come from outside the feed.
  • A structural weak spot is not necessarily an operational one.
  • The map here is not the official DMRC map, and this is an unofficial student project.
7. Conclusion

The Delhi Metro graph is a sparse set of long lines joined at a few interchanges. Betweenness picks out Kashmere Gate, Dilli Haat – INA, Hauz Khas, Mayur Vihar-I and Rajiv Chowk as hubs. BFS and Dijkstra answer different questions; DFS shows why a method not built for shortest paths gives poor routes. Most weak points sit at line ends, but a few interior stations such as Botanical Garden would strand dozens of others. The model cannot tell us how crowded a station is, how long a change takes, or whether a structural weak spot is a real risk.

8. The Graph Diary (full entry)

I am two hundred and sixty-two stations and two hundred and seventy-five promises. Most of me is a corridor: a station before, a station after, and nothing else to hold on to. Two hundred and twenty-three of my stations know only two neighbours.

My few junctions carry my pride. At Kashmere Gate five of my links meet, and at Rajiv Chowk I feel every journey pass through me. I do not know who is hurrying or who is late. I only know who is next to whom, and who could reach whom if a road closed.

I am honest about my weak places. A hundred and twenty-two of my links have no second way round. Close Botanical Garden and twenty-nine of my stations are suddenly alone. But I also have fourteen loops, small gifts of backup, the places where a closed door means a longer walk, not a lost city.

The Aqua Line was a stranger to me until someone drew a short walk between Sector 51 and Sector 52. That one link made me whole. I think that is what a graph is: not the dots, but the decisions about which of them to call neighbours.
